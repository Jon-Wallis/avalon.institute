WRITE A NEO-FUTURIST PLAY FOR AVALON!

Avalon is soliciting 1-second to 2-minute-long micro-plays for a fully staged, live theatrical production this fall!

Plays will be loosely inspired by the theme, “How Fast Should We Go?”

Interpret this theme how you like; write about romantic escalation, AI development, tectonic plates shifting or the process of removing a booger from your nose. We want comedy, drama, dance/movement, music, philosophy, avant-garde mooing, personal revelation… whatever excites you.

What is this? Why are the plays so short?!

In the Neo-Futurist theater style, developed in the 80’s in Chicago, a troupe of actors attempts to perform 30 tiny plays in 60 minutes (and it used to be that if they failed, they’d buy the audience pizza). Because of this inherent time pressure, the Neo-Futurist form tends to be brief, honest, immediate and intimate, often breaking the fourth wall or allowing the actors to play themselves.

Writing for this format is usually not a long, laborious process of invention, with 3-dimensional characters and complete plotlines, like writing a full-length or even a ten-minute play. A “play” in this sense might be a song, an interesting stage direction the actors get to interpret, or a two-line joke. We strongly encourage you to hold it lightly.

The easiest way to get a sense of this style is to check out the linked example plays!

Submissions are due August 31st. First priority will be given to plays submitted earlier. There is no limit on the number of submissions. Submit to theater@avalon.institute

Why so soon??!
In keeping with the spirit of speed and play, we’re not giving you too long to deliberate! We hope you’ll hold it lightly and maybe send us multiple plays, or even drafts. There will be time for workshopping with the creative team.

Once submissions close, 30 micro-plays will be chosen for a fully staged production this October. We’ll be casting professional actors to perform your work.

Have fun!

Notes On Form

Your play can be 1 second to 2.5 minutes long. If it’s longer, we probably won’t be able to include it, so try to keep it short (don’t forget action and lived-in dialogue usually takes longer than reading something aloud).

  • If you’re not used to writing for the stage, check out the idea of “theatricality” by David Ball to get your juices flowing.

  • This is an experimental art form! We encourage you to experiment with theatrical elements beyond traditional dialogue, character, and plot. Some options:

    • lighting

    • props or costumes

    • movement, dance, or bizarre physicality (ex: “all 5 actors dance erratically for 10 seconds, then stop abruptly” or “the actor now tries to hang a spoon on their nose and keep it there for 5 seconds. If he fails, another actor dumps a bucket of ice water on their head. If he succeeds, he heartily shakes the hand of an audience member. He then then offers her the spoon.”)

    • architecture & environment (the walls, the floor, the ceiling, the lobby, San Francisco streets, etc)

    • audience interaction (“breaking the fourth wall”)

    • improvisation prompts for the actors (“Actor 1 now tells a real, 10 second story from his life that day”)

    • soundscapes & music

    • multi-media

  • If you want to write a song or a musical number, we’d love that! We’ll then cast the ensemble with the necessary skills in mind.

  • All this said, if you want to write something more traditional, go for it! We want that too.

  • A script is a skeleton; don’t be afraid to leave some space for creative interpretation. The actors and director will have a blast figuring out what to do with a more open-ended prompt, as long as there’s some communicative vision underlying it. Shakespeare famously just writes “they fight” when they, well, fight– and the director and actors craft a whole movement piece out of that simple instruction.

As you might imagine, the show will be slightly different each night due to elements of chance, surprise, improv, and audience engagement.

Notes On Content

How tightly should I hold the theme of “How Fast Should We Go?”

Loosely. If this prompt does something for you, awesome– if not, ignore it. Write what’s on the edge of your thinking or feeling these days. Writing from your curiosity, pain, delight, or intellectual excitement is much more likely to create a play we want to see. We’re looking for a mosaic of experiences that form a poetic, moving, insightful, oddball or humorous dialectic– not a clear, linear thematic arc. Make us laugh. Make us think. Surprise yourself. And please, play with the form of theatre! This isn’t a substack post! If we don’t think the play you submitted fits in the show, but we enjoyed your risk-taking, maybe we’ll ask you to write a different one.

Still confused about the Neo-Futurist aesthetic? The Neos’ website puts it clearly…..

You: I have a monologue that I wrote in which I reveal to my sister…

Neos: Whoa – your sister’s coming to your play?

You: Well, no.

Neos: You’re going to call your sister during your play?

You: No.

Neos: Sounds like what you’ve written is probably not in the Neo-Futurist aesthetic. Call your sister and put her on speakerphone for us instead!
